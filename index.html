Case study · Southern California Edison · 2024 to present

Retiring a legacy orchestration layer

A Python integration pipeline now runs the HR onboarding and technical provisioning that a legacy orchestration layer used to handle, for more than 4,000 action requests a month.

When someone joins, HR opens an onboarding request that lists what they need, such as a laptop, software, a phone or a hotspot. The pipeline turns each request into one ticket per item in the IT service management system, then tracks every ticket until the system issues its request number.

A run has three steps. Post creates the tickets. Fetch asks for the request number of each ticket that is still waiting, and saves the full record once it exists. Sync copies the matching request records into a reporting table. Running it again is safe, because a ticket that already exists is never posted twice and anything still waiting is picked up next time.

Most of the design effort went into failure. If a ticket is created but cannot be saved, the run stops so that nothing exists that nothing tracks. An error is never read as no answer. A ticket whose request was handled by hand is checked once more and then closed, and an empty read never closes anything. A sanity check and a set of repair tools report first and change data only when switched on.

It reaches the ticketing system through SOAP and a custom REST API and writes normalized records to SQL Server, which removed a polling dependency. After a formal architecture review it was rebuilt as an Azure-native service, with containerized job execution and secrets held in Key Vault behind a managed identity. A Power Automate cloud flow triggers a desktop flow that runs it unattended each morning.

  • 4,000+action requests a month no longer depend on the legacy layer.
  • 354automated tests, with the two external systems replaced by fakes so a test run never touches production.
  • 42.9%fewer monthly data defects after 22 validation checks across SAP, Azure and Power BI.
  • 89%SLA adherence, up from 78%, with the backlog down 31% after workflow reporting was standardized.
  • 32%improvement in triage time from a Power BI dashboard on exceptions and failure reasons.
  • 35%fewer manual follow-ups once Power Automate took over more than 60,000 line items a month.

Case study · Swiggy · Hyderabad hub

Cloud kitchens where demand had no supply

Mapping competitors' exclusive restaurants against our own orders showed where Swiggy had demand but no supply. Cloud kitchens filled those gaps, and customers who ordered least often ordered more once they could see them.

Zomato and Uber Eats were handling more orders than Swiggy in Hyderabad. Our business strategy team proposed testing cloud kitchens, starting at the Hyderabad hub, and I worked on it as a senior business strategy analyst.

  1. Map the competitionWe collected the restaurants that were exclusive to competitors from their websites, took their latitude and longitude, and plotted them in Google My Maps.
  2. Lay our orders over the mapWhere competitors were strong we had few orders. Customers there preferred a restaurant that only our competitors carried.
  3. Check how far orders travelledWe geocoded customer addresses from our internal data and plotted them against the restaurants they ordered from. Many orders came from a long way away.
  4. Fill the gapWe opened cloud kitchens in areas with high demand and low supply. Long-distance orders fell, and orders rose in those areas and in the ones beside them.
  5. Take it back to the restaurantsWe showed competitor-exclusive restaurants the demand data and the order counts from our cloud kitchens. They were interested, and we brought some onto Swiggy through cloud kitchens. That created supply where there had been none.
  6. Test itOne group of customers could see cloud kitchens in the app and a control group could not. Before splitting them, we matched the groups on orders per week, orders per weekend and basket size, so the two started out alike.

Case study · Grocery Outlet · Internship, 2022

A performance scorecard for independent operators

Five pillars give every independent operator's store one scorecard, so a store can be compared with its peers and the pillar that needs work can be named.

Operator performance was judged through manager feedback on merchandising, overall competency and marketing, alongside store performance and operating cost dashboards. My final internship project set out a common measure for it, the operator performance metric and a year-over-year scorecard.

Working with data in Amazon Redshift and MicroStrategy, I looked for the correlations between store measures. I grouped the data by department, by state and financial region, and by quarter, and split stores by state and sales-volume band.

Case study · Taksy Kraft Info Technologies · Startup, India

MyRyd and Cabdekho, two products for cab passengers

An early-stage team set out to make time in a cab useful, with entertainment, security and offers for passengers, paid for by advertisers and cab promotions.

I was part of the founding team as a business strategist.

MyRyd put a tablet in the cab. Trips in traffic average 45 minutes to an hour, and apart from the radio nothing entertains the passenger. The product added entertainment, security and guidance, with a focus on the safety of women traveling in cabs, and location-based advertising to pay for it. It was aimed first at premium cars serving hotels.

Cabdekho was the companion app. It showed the shortest route with relative fares, compared the prices of cabs near the rider, showed the current surge ratio and the time for a cab to arrive, and listed offers in the city.

The go-to-market plan began with campus ambassadors, workshops and fest registrations through the app at India's top universities, on a budget of Rs 2 per download. On the supply side the team contacted more than 200 drivers from Ola, Uber and Meru, approached local advertisers, and built a list of 780 senior executives to pitch. The market was sized across hotel, commercial and corporate cabs.

Term paper and prototype · Earlier work

An automatic SOS system for car crashes

A crash sensed by a MEMS accelerometer sends an SOS message on its own, with the accident's location, time and date, so rescuers do not depend on the passengers to call.

After a heavy impact, passengers can be badly hurt or unconscious, and on a remote highway there may be no way to reach rescuers. Delay in first aid can cost lives. The paper sets out a device that removes that dependence, and a prototype of it was built.

The design pairs an ADXL001 MEMS accelerometer with GPS, GSM and an ARM processor. On a crash it sends an SMS with the latitude and longitude, the name of the place, the time and the date to the required authorities and to a government-monitored head office. The head office confirms delivery and keeps the record for later legal reference. Meanwhile a siren sounds and the high beams flash.

Research · In preparation for ACM Journal of Data and Information Quality

Stage-aware data validation, tested against seeded defects

The paper asks whether rule-based and language-model methods can generate data validation checks and place each one at the right stage of a pipeline, scored by running the checks against defects seeded into the data on purpose.

It is pre-registered. The hypotheses, the comparison against a profiler baseline and the analysis plan are fixed before any scored run, and every departure from the plan goes into a dated amendment log. Open-weight models run locally and Claude models run through a headless client, all under one harness that is written in standard-library Python with automated tests.

The design separates four questions. Can a method extract the rules from documentation? Can it classify a check and choose its stage? Does its confidence tell you when to route a case to a person? Does placing a check at the right stage catch more defects? No scored run has happened yet, so no results are reported here.

LinkedIn · 2025

Scoring Trust: How Credit Risk Principles Shape Fraud Detection in Last-Mile Delivery

Fraud is not industry-specific. It is behavior-specific, and the question is the same everywhere: can I trust this transaction right now?

Drawing on my fraud and risk work at Swiggy, the post compares how banks and delivery platforms score risk. Banks judge creditworthiness over months or years. Delivery platforms judge moments of trust hundreds of times a day. Its lesson is that the best risk systems prevent fraud instead of detecting it, because fraud in delivery is a trust problem disguised as a transaction.

Banking and credit cards

  • Repayment behavior
  • Credit utilization and capacity
  • Risk profiling and scoring
  • Account tenure and depth

Predict the ability and willingness to repay over time.

Last-mile delivery

  • Digital identity and device signals
  • Transaction behavior and timing
  • Anomaly and abuse detection
  • Location and delivery integrity

Determine intent and legitimacy in real time, often within milliseconds.
